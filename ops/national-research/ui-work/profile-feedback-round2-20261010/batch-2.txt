51|This ambrosia beetle is a small, slender, light-brown insect found in wood. Euwallacea similis has been recorded across parts of Asia and the Pacific, where it uses weakened trees and timber. Details on the ends of its wing covers help specialists distinguish it from related beetles. That distinction matters: the tree diseases and damage associated with other Euwallacea species should not automatically be attributed to this one.
52|American evergreen is a familiar houseplant with arrow-shaped young leaves. As it grows and climbs, its leaves become more deeply divided. In warm places such as Florida, escaped plants can spread across the ground and climb trees, covering other vegetation. Pieces of stem can take root, so discarded garden material can help this tropical vine move beyond the place where it was planted.
53|Caprella scaura looks more like a tiny walking stick than a shrimp. Its slender body and grasping limbs let it hold onto seaweed and the growth on docks, ropes and boat hulls. These surfaces also give it a way to travel between harbors. Where it becomes abundant, it can compete with other small marine animals for space and food, although its original native range remains uncertain.
54|Amur corktree is a deciduous tree with deeply ridged bark and leaves made up of several leaflets. Female trees produce dark berries that can remain into winter, giving birds a meal and a way to spread the seeds. Introduced as an ornamental, it has established in parts of the northern United States. Its seedlings can grow beneath other trees, allowing it to enter forest understories.
55|Amur goby is a small East Asian fish that lives close to the bottom of streams and other freshwater habitats. Males guard eggs laid beneath stones. Fish identified as Rhinogobius brunneus have been recorded in the northwestern United States, but the name has historically covered several closely related forms. That makes careful identification important when interpreting records or assessing this fish's effects in a new place.
56|Amur honeysuckle is a large shrub with paired, pointed leaves, white flowers that turn yellow, and bright red berries. Birds eat the fruit and spread its seeds. It leafs out early and often stays green late in the season, giving it a long growing period. Dense stands can shade the forest floor and leave less room and light for native plants.
57|Anchorworm is a crustacean, despite its wormlike appearance. The adult female attaches to a fish with an anchor-shaped structure embedded in its skin, leaving much of her long body exposed. It affects fish including carp, koi and goldfish, especially in warm water. Attachment sites can become wounds, and heavy infestations can weaken fish. Moving infested fish can introduce the parasite to new ponds.
58|Anchored waterhyacinth grows from roots in the mud, with long stems extending through the water and clusters of blue to mauve flowers above it. Unlike the familiar floating waterhyacinth, it stays attached to the bottom and lacks the same swollen leaf stalks. This plant comes from tropical America and grows in slow-moving water, where its trailing stems can form dense patches.
59|Angel's tears is a shrub or small tree best recognized by its enormous, hanging trumpet-shaped flowers. Native to Brazil, it is widely grown in warm-climate gardens and can persist or escape outside cultivation. The flowers make it striking, but this is a poisonous member of the nightshade family. Its ornamental appeal should not be mistaken for suitability as an edible plant.
60|Angel's-wings is a cactus whose paired, oval pads often resemble rabbit ears. The pads carry clusters of tiny, barbed bristles rather than the obvious long spines of many other cacti; these bristles can irritate skin. Yellow flowers may be followed by purple fruit. Fallen pads can take root, helping garden escapes form stands, including in parts of Australia where it is considered invasive.
61|Angleton bluestem is a warm-season perennial grass grown for forage. It forms upright clumps with several narrow flowering branches spreading from near the top of each stem. Fine hairs around the flowers are useful identification details. It has been introduced into the southern United States and other warm regions for pasture, and can also occur outside planted fields.
62|Angoumois grain moth spends its most damaging stage hidden inside a grain kernel. Its larvae feed within crops such as wheat and corn, including grain in storage. Adults are small, pale moths with narrow, fringed wings and do not feed. Because the larvae are concealed, infested grain may look largely intact until a moth emerges through a small hole.
63|Angular mosaic of beans is associated with cowpea mild mottle virus, which infects legumes including cowpea and soybean. Leaves may develop uneven yellowing or become distorted, although some infected plants show few obvious symptoms. Whiteflies can spread the virus between plants. Similar-looking leaf damage has many possible causes, so a visual inspection alone cannot confirm an infection.
64|Animated oat is a wild annual grass with long, bent bristles extending from its seeds. These bristles, called awns, are one of its most noticeable features. Despite the scientific name Avena sterilis, it produces seed. It grows in fields and disturbed ground and can spread with contaminated crop seed. Its wild relatives also interest crop breeders looking for useful traits such as disease resistance.
65|Anise, listed here as Myrrhis odorata, is a perennial herb with finely divided leaves, clusters of small white flowers and a strong anise-like scent. Long grown as a culinary herb, it has escaped cultivation in parts of North America. It is also known as sweet cicely, a name shared with native North American plants that belong to a different genus.
66|Annual bastardcabbage is a tall mustard with branching stems and small yellow flowers. It comes from parts of Europe, North Africa and Asia and grows along roads and in fields. In places such as Texas, it can also form dense patches in natural areas. Its distinctive fruits help distinguish it from other yellow-flowered mustards, which can look similar from a distance.
67|Annual bluegrass is a small, light-green grass often found in lawns, paths and other frequently disturbed places. It can produce pale seed heads even when kept short by mowing. Its leaf tips are shaped a little like the bow of a boat. It grows well in cool conditions and produces seed quickly; despite its name, some forms can survive for more than one season.
68|Annual canarygrass is grown for the seed commonly used in birdseed mixes. Its upright stems carry compact, oval flower heads with overlapping, striped green and pale coverings. Plants can appear along roads, in fields and near places where seed has been spilled. It is a different species from reed canarygrass, the perennial wetland grass often discussed in invasive-plant management.
69|Annual candytuft has clusters of white or pale violet flowers, each with two petals larger than the others. As the stems lengthen, the flowers give way to small, flattened, winged seed pods. This garden plant comes from Europe and North Africa and sometimes grows outside cultivation. An escaped plant is not automatically an invasive one; its behavior depends on the location.
70|Annual fescue is a slender grass with narrow leaves and a long seed head lined with fine bristles. Its small size can make it easy to overlook among other grasses. It grows in disturbed ground and can become a weed in crops, including wheat fields in the Pacific Northwest. The timing of seed dormancy and germination helps it persist through the farming cycle.
71|Annual hedgenettle is a member of the mint family with paired, toothed leaves and small, pale flowers shaped like open lips. Unlike many familiar mints, it usually completes its life cycle in a single season. Native to parts of Europe and western Asia, it also occurs in North American fields and other disturbed ground. Its flowers and leaf arrangement help distinguish it from nearby weeds.
72|Annual mercury is a branching plant with toothed green leaves and small, inconspicuous flowers. Male flowers are held on narrow spikes, while female flowers sit closer to the leaf joints, often on separate plants. It grows in cultivated and disturbed ground and completes its life cycle in one season. The flower arrangement is more useful for identification than its otherwise fairly ordinary green leaves.
73|Annual rabbitsfootgrass gets its name from its soft-looking, fuzzy flower heads. The dense clusters are pale green at first and turn straw-colored as they mature, with fine bristles giving them their distinctive texture. It commonly grows in damp, disturbed places such as ditches, pond margins and irrigated fields. Although it may form thick patches, it is an annual that returns from seed.
74|Annual ryegrass is a fast-growing grass used for forage and cover crops. Its glossy leaves clasp the stem, and its seeds develop along a narrow spike, often with bristles projecting from the flower clusters. It can also become a troublesome weed in grain fields. Despite the name, some plants live into a second season, and closely related ryegrasses can be difficult to tell apart.
75|Annual wallrocket begins with a cluster of toothed leaves near the ground. Slender, mostly leafless stems rise above them and carry small yellow flowers with four petals. It grows in disturbed places, including paths, field margins and around walls. Originally from parts of Europe and nearby regions, it has been introduced to North America, where it can resemble several other small mustard-family weeds.
76|Annual yellow sweetclover has leaves divided into three leaflets and narrow clusters of tiny yellow flowers. These develop into small, rounded pods with visible veins. It grows in disturbed ground and cultivated settings, including orchards, vineyards and grain fields. Its modest flower size helps distinguish it from larger sweetclovers, though a close look at the pods is often useful.
77|Tapinoma litorale is a tiny ant that often nests above the ground in hollow twigs and vines. Small colonies can be easy to miss until their nesting material is disturbed. Available research places it within the native fauna of Florida and the West Indies. Its appearance in an invasive-species catalog should therefore not be treated as evidence that it is introduced or harmful everywhere it occurs.
78|Anoecia vagans is an aphid with a life cycle involving dogwoods and grass roots. Its pale green, yellow or olive coloring can vary with its stage of life. Root-feeding colonies are less visible than aphids on leaves, and ants may attend them for the sugary liquid they produce. Knowing the host plant and season is especially useful when trying to identify this small insect.
79|Aphis eugeniae is a small aphid recorded on several shrubs and herbs, particularly members of the spurge family. It is known from parts of Asia and the Pacific, with records also reported from Florida. It resembles other aphids, including the spirea aphid, so the plant it is feeding on is only one clue. A reliable identification may require examination of fine body features.
80|Asiphonella dactylonii feeds on the roots and lower parts of grasses, including bermudagrass. Its pale body may be covered with bluish-white wax, and colonies can remain hidden near or below the soil surface. That makes it less conspicuous than aphids clustered on shoots. Records establish its association with grasses, but do not by themselves show that it causes serious damage wherever it is found.
81|Cerataphis lataniae is an aphid that can look surprisingly like a scale insect. Its body is flattened and oval, with a fringe of white wax around the edge. It feeds on palms and belongs to a group whose members are easily confused, especially with Cerataphis brasiliensis. A photograph may show the waxy outline clearly without revealing enough detail to identify the species confidently.
82|Dysaphis crataegi is associated with hawthorns and plants such as carrots, where colonies may occur around the roots or crown. Its yellowish to gray-green body can have a waxy coating, and ants may gather around colonies to collect honeydew. Several related aphids share similar features. The host plant, feeding location and microscopic details all help separate this species from its lookalikes.
83|Eucallipterus tiliae feeds beneath the leaves of lindens and basswoods. The aphids are yellow with dark markings, but people often notice their sticky honeydew before seeing the insects themselves. It can coat cars, benches and other surfaces below an affected tree and support dark sooty mold. This species is known from Europe and North America and is closely associated with its linden hosts.
84|Geoica setulosa is a pale aphid that feeds on grass roots. Ants tend its colonies for honeydew and may build small soil mounds around them. In turf, these mounds can be more noticeable than the aphids themselves and may cover or smother nearby grass. Understanding the relationship between the two insects helps explain damage that might otherwise be attributed entirely to root feeding.
85|Geopemphigus floccosus is a root-feeding aphid associated with plants in the morning-glory genus, Ipomoea. Its pale green or yellow body can be covered in wax, and its underground feeding habits make it easy to overlook. Research describes an American origin, but parts of its life cycle remain poorly documented. Its presence should be interpreted carefully rather than assumed to represent an invasive pest outbreak.
86|Greenidea ficicola feeds on young leaves and shoots of figs, including ornamental Ficus plants. Its green to brown body has unusually long, curved tubes projecting from the rear, a useful clue for recognition. Native to Asia, it has been introduced to places including Florida. Colonies may affect ornamental growth, though it is generally described as a relatively minor pest in the available regional accounts.
87|Hyalopterus amygdali is a slender, green aphid often covered with pale, mealy-looking wax. It is associated with almonds and can use reeds during part of its seasonal cycle. Closely related aphids look very similar and may occur on related host plants. For that reason, the host alone is not a reliable identification, and specialists sometimes use genetic evidence to distinguish members of this group.
88|Hyperomyzus lactucae can curl and yellow currant leaves in spring before moving to sowthistles, where it feeds on stems and flower shoots. In warmer regions, colonies may remain on these herbaceous hosts. It is a green aphid and can transmit certain plant viruses. Finding the insect is not the same as confirming a virus infection, since leaf symptoms can have several causes.
89|Hyperomyzus picridis is a glossy yellow-green aphid with darker markings and tubes extending from the rear of its body. Its hosts include alpine currant, oxtongues and hawksbeards. Colonies may gather just below flower heads, where tender growth offers a feeding site. Host associations and small anatomical differences are important for identification because several related aphids have a similar overall appearance.
90|Macrosiphoniella tanacetaria feeds on tansy, often gathering around its upper stems and flower heads. The aphids are gray-green with a waxy coating and dark legs, antennae and rear tubes. Unlike aphids that move between trees and herbs, this species can spend its seasonal cycle on herbaceous plants. It has been introduced to North America, where tansy provides a recognizable place to look for it.
91|Macrosiphum lilii is an aphid associated with lilies. It can be pale yellow or orange, sometimes with a purple patch, and has long, dark legs and tubes at the rear of its body. Feeding colonies produce honeydew, which can make nearby plant surfaces sticky. Its seasonal life cycle is not well documented in the available accounts, so host and appearance provide a better starting point than assumptions about its movements.
92|Melanaphis sorini is an aphid associated with Miscanthus grasses. Individuals may be tan to wine-purple, and the species has been recorded in Asia as well as California and Florida. It belongs to a genus that also contains important crop pests, but those relatives' impacts should not be assigned to this species. Identifying the grass host and examining the aphid closely are important first steps.
93|Metopolophium festucae is a grass-feeding aphid that may be green, yellow or pink. Winged individuals can have dark bands on the body. It belongs to a group in which the subspecies matters: the cereal-feeding form, Metopolophium festucae cerealium, has its own records and research history. A broad identification to species should not automatically be treated as evidence for that particular crop-associated form.
94|Metopolophium festucae cerealium is a pale, elongated aphid found on cereal crops such as wheat and barley. Winged individuals have dark body bands that can help draw attention to them among other aphids. It has been recorded in the Pacific Northwest and Kansas. Research has examined its reproduction and effects on crops, but the results vary with conditions rather than supporting a single damage estimate for every field.
95|Myzaphis rosarum is a tiny yellow to green aphid found on roses and shrubby cinquefoil. It often feeds along veins on young or unfolding leaves. Short antennae and slightly swollen tubes at the rear of the body help distinguish it under magnification. It is less often associated with severe outbreaks than some familiar rose aphids, though its small size can make colonies easy to overlook.
96|Myzus varians feeds on peach leaves, which may curl or roll around colonies. It is usually green, with dark tips on the tubes projecting from its rear, and may move to clematis during its life cycle. The species can transmit plum pox virus, but its presence alone does not establish that a tree is infected. Both insect identification and plant diagnosis require more than curled leaves.
97|Pleotrichophorus chrysanthemi is a pale yellow-green aphid found beneath chrysanthemum leaves. Feeding may be associated with yellowing, including on plants grown under greenhouse conditions. Its identity is complicated by questions about how it differs from a close relative. That makes a careful identification especially important when comparing records, and prevents every similar aphid on a chrysanthemum from being treated as this species.
98|Rhopalomyzus lonicerae has different-looking colonies at different points in its life cycle. Dark green aphids feed on honeysuckle in spring, where leaves may roll around them. Later, paler colonies can occur on grasses, including reed canarygrass in wet places. This seasonal change in host and appearance is useful context: two colonies that look different may still belong to the same species.
99|Rhopalosiphum rufiabdominale is commonly associated with roots, where its colonies are easily missed. The aphids are greenish to brown, sometimes with a rusty area toward the rear, and use grasses and other plants, including greenhouse crops. Their hidden feeding habits can complicate diagnosis. Poor root growth alone is not enough to identify them; the insects themselves need to be found and examined.
100|Sipha glyceriae is an aphid of wet places, feeding on grasses, rushes and sedges. Its green body may show a reddish stripe and lighter markings along the back. It uses herbaceous hosts through its seasonal cycle rather than relying on an obvious tree-to-grass migration. Looking closely at wetland vegetation can reveal colonies that would otherwise blend into their surroundings.
