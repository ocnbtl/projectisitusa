151|Asian copperleaf is an annual plant with upright stems and toothed, oval leaves. Young growth may be reddish. Broad, heart-shaped structures surround the small flowers and developing fruit, providing a useful identification feature. Native to Asia, it has been recorded as a weed in parts of the American Midwest, where it can grow and produce seed beneath a crop canopy.
152|Asian drill snail is a marine snail with a thick, ridged shell, often around one to two inches long. It lives around shell, gravel and muddy oyster habitats and feeds by boring through the shells of other animals. Native to the northwestern Pacific, it has traveled with oyster shipments. Its feeding makes it a concern where introduced populations overlap with shellfish beds.
153|Asian flatsedge is an annual sedge of muddy river margins, ditches and fields. Its narrow leaves grow below branched flower clusters surrounded by longer, leaf-like bracts. The small yellow-brown scales in those clusters have green, pointed midribs. Originally associated with East Asia, it has also been recorded as a field weed in Europe. Close flower details help distinguish it from similar sedges.
154|Asian kelp is the brown seaweed widely known as wakame. It has a broad blade with a clear central rib and a ruffled reproductive structure near the base. It grows on rocks as well as ropes and boat hulls, which can help it reach new coasts. Cultivated as food, it is also invasive in some temperate regions, where dense growth can change the habitat available to other marine life.
155|Asian longhorned beetle is a large black beetle with white spots and long, banded antennae. Its larvae tunnel through hardwood trees, including maples, and can eventually kill them. Round exit holes, chewed bark and coarse sawdust-like material can reveal an infestation. Native to Asia, it has reached other regions through wood packaging, making careful identification of beetles and tree damage especially important.
156|Asian marsh snail is a tiny brown snail with a blunt, cone-shaped shell, reaching only about five millimeters. It lives high in salt or brackish marshes, where the ground is not continuously underwater. Native to Japan and Korea, it has also been recorded in Oregon. Available Smithsonian accounts do not report observed impacts there, so its introduction should be distinguished from demonstrated ecological harm.
157|Asian marshweed changes its appearance above and below water. Submerged leaves are finely divided and arranged around the stem, while leaves above the surface are firmer and toothed. Small pink to violet flowers may appear above the water. Introduced through the aquarium trade, it has established in the southeastern United States, where rooting stem fragments and dense growth can help it crowd other aquatic plants.
158|Asian mustard begins as a rough-leaved rosette before sending up branching stems with small, pale yellow flowers and long seed pods. In southwestern North America, it can grow quickly after winter rain and compete with native desert wildflowers. It comes from the Mediterranean region and nearby parts of Asia. Its ability to spread through open desert habitat makes it more than an ordinary roadside weed in affected areas.
159|Asian nakedwood is a sprawling evergreen shrub with shiny, toothed leaves, small greenish-yellow flowers and rounded seed capsules. Its seeds can float and tolerate salty conditions, helping it move through coastal environments. Native to parts of the Old World tropics, it has become invasive in Florida's coastal habitats, where its climbing and spreading growth can cover other vegetation.
160|Asian needle ant is a dark ant with paler legs and antenna tips. It often nests in damp wood, leaf litter or beneath stones and feeds on termites and other small animals. Established in parts of the eastern United States, it can displace native ants, including species that disperse plant seeds. It also has a painful sting. Newer references usually call it Brachyponera chinensis.
161|Asian pigeonwings is a tropical vine with striking blue flowers that have pale centers. Its leaves are divided into oval leaflets, and flat pods follow the flowers. Widely known as butterfly pea, it is grown ornamentally and for flowers used to color foods and drinks. It can live as a perennial in warm climates, climbing over nearby supports and vegetation.
162|Asian ponysfoot is a low, creeping plant with small kidney-shaped leaves. Its stems spread across the ground, forming a mat, and its tiny greenish to white flowers are easy to overlook. It has been used as a lawn substitute. Fine hairs and other small features help distinguish it from related Dichondra species; the common name alone is not a reliable guide to its native range.
163|Asian shore crab has a square shell with three small teeth along each front side. It shelters beneath rocks in the intertidal zone and eats a variety of plant and animal foods. Native to the western Pacific, it has established along parts of the eastern United States. High numbers can compete with other crabs and affect the small animals living on rocky shores.
164|Asian soybean rust is a fungal disease that can cause tan or reddish leaf spots and tiny spore-producing bumps, especially on leaf undersides. Wind carries the spores between living host plants, including soybean and kudzu. Warm areas with suitable hosts can help the fungus survive between crop seasons. Under favorable wet conditions, severe infections can cause early leaf loss and reduce seed production.
165|Asian spiderflower is a sticky, hairy annual with yellow flowers and leaves often divided into five leaflets. Long, narrow seed pods develop along the stems, and plants can reach roughly three feet tall. The glandular hairs are a useful clue when distinguishing it from other yellow-flowered weeds. Older references use the name Cleome viscosa, while this catalog lists it as Arivela viscosa.
166|Asian swamp eel is an elongated fish with smooth, scaleless skin, small eyes and greatly reduced fins. It can breathe air and use muddy, low-oxygen habitats that challenge many other fish. It eats aquatic animals and has spread through trade and releases. The name Monopterus albus has covered a complex of related forms, so records under that name may need closer taxonomic review.
167|Asian swordfern has long, arching fronds made up of many narrow leaflets. Flat, dark brown scales on the leaf stalks are among the features used to distinguish it from similar ferns. It can form dense stands in sheltered places and spreads through spores. In Florida, introduced populations can displace native vegetation, making accurate identification important where several swordferns grow together.
168|Asian tapeworm is a parasite that lives in the intestines of freshwater fish. It infects carp, minnows and many other hosts, and can reduce growth or harm young fish. Moving infected fish, including bait, can help it reach new waters. It is not identified by the fish's appearance alone; examining the parasite or using genetic methods is needed to distinguish it reliably.
169|Asian tiger mosquito is black with white-banded legs and a single white stripe down its back. It bites during the day and breeds in small water-filled containers such as tires, buckets and plant trays. Eggs laid just above the waterline can survive dry periods. That combination has helped this Asian species travel with goods and establish around homes in many other regions.
170|Asian tiger shrimp is a large shrimp with bold bands across its body. Young animals use estuaries and mangroves, while adults breed in marine waters and feed near the bottom, often at night. Native to the Indo-Pacific and widely farmed for food, it has been found along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts of the United States. Its presence raises questions about effects on native species, which require local evidence.
171|Asian waterwort is a tiny, creeping plant of wet mud and shallow water. Small leaves and flowers grow along its delicate stems, often making it inconspicuous among larger wetland plants. It occurs in places such as rice fields and river margins. Several waterworts are similar, so flower and seed details can be important when deciding whether a record truly belongs to this species.
172|Asiatic clam is a freshwater clam with a rounded, roughly triangular shell marked by strong ridges. Shells range from yellowish to brown. It filters particles from the water and can reach high densities, changing food and nutrient conditions and competing with native mussels. Large populations may also clog water-intake equipment, making it important to both aquatic ecosystems and water infrastructure.
173|Asiatic dayflower opens vivid blue flowers that usually last only a day. Each flower has two large blue petals and a much smaller pale one. Its leaves wrap around the stems, which can take root where they touch the ground. Native to East Asia, it grows in disturbed places and can become a crop weed, spreading through patches of moist, suitable soil.
174|Asiatic dock is a perennial with large lower leaves and tall flowering stems. It grows in places such as river margins, meadows and disturbed fields. Both seeds and spreading roots can help it establish dense patches. It has expanded in parts of Europe outside its earlier range, where its size and persistent root growth can make established stands difficult to displace.
175|Asiatic garden beetle is a small, cinnamon-brown beetle with a rounded shape resembling a coffee bean. Adults hide during the day and feed on leaves at night. Underground, the pale, C-shaped grubs feed on roots and can damage seedlings. Introduced from Asia, it occurs in the United States, where the larvae have also become a concern in some corn-growing areas.
176|Asiatic witchweed is a parasitic plant that attaches to the roots of grasses, including crops such as corn and sorghum. Its narrow, hairy leaves and small flowers appear above ground, while the connection to its host remains hidden below. Seeds can persist in soil for a long time. That persistence helps explain the long-running monitoring and control efforts around infestations in the Carolinas.
177|Asparagus beetle is a small, dark beetle with pale spots and reddish edges. Both adults and grayish larvae feed on asparagus. Dark eggs are often laid upright on the plant, and feeding can scar spears or strip foliage. It differs from the orange, spotted asparagus beetle, so noticing the adult's color pattern helps connect damage with the correct species.
178|Asparagus fern has fine, feathery-looking growth, but it is a flowering plant rather than a true fern. What look like tiny leaves are modified stems. Small pale flowers can be followed by red berries, and cultivated forms range from upright plumes to arching sprays. Names are sometimes confused with the related Asparagus aethiopicus, so garden labels alone may not establish the exact species.
179|Aspen leafblotch miner spends its larval stage inside the leaves of aspens and cottonwoods. Early feeding uses sap, while later feeding removes internal leaf tissue and produces a visible blotch. Silk can draw the mined area into a raised fold. The adult moth is tiny, so the pale, altered patches on leaves are often a more noticeable sign than the insect itself.
180|This asthmaweed, Conyza bonariensis, is an annual or short-lived plant with narrow, hairy, gray-green leaves. Its many small flower heads release seeds carried by fine, fluffy hairs. Originally from South America, it grows in fields, orchards and along roads in other regions. Wind dispersal helps it reach newly disturbed ground, where a small plant can develop into a much-branched seed producer.
181|Conyza floribunda is one of several plants called asthmaweed. It starts with leaves near the ground, then develops leafy stems carrying many small flower heads. Fine bristles help its seeds travel on the wind. Records include openings in coastal live-oak habitat in Texas. References may place these plants in Conyza or Erigeron, making scientific-name cross-checks useful when comparing information about them.
182|Athel tamarisk is a large evergreen tree with fine, jointed-looking branches and tiny, scale-like leaves. Its deep roots and tolerance of dry, salty conditions have made it useful for shelter plantings. Outside cultivation, it can spread along waterways. In parts of Australia, dense stands have altered river habitats and soil conditions, showing how a tree valued for protection from wind can become a problem in the wrong setting.
183|Atlantic ivy is an evergreen vine that can carpet the ground or climb trees. Young growth has lobed leaves, while mature flowering growth has less-divided leaves and produces fruit eaten by birds. Stems can also root as they spread. Introduced populations in the Pacific Northwest can dominate forest ground cover and add weight to trees. It is closely related to, but distinct from, English ivy.
184|Aucuba whitefly is a tiny sap-feeding insect that uses many host plants, including citrus and ivy. Its dark, oval immature stages sit on leaves and are easier to examine with magnification. Plants moved through trade can carry it between locations. A California assessment reported no significant known impacts at that time, so detection should be distinguished from evidence of serious damage.
185|Australasian geranium is a low-growing perennial with hairy, rounded leaves divided into lobes. Its small pink flowers have paler centers and usually appear singly or in pairs. Thickened roots support the plant in moist places such as gullies, creek margins and forested ground. The modest flowers and creeping or spreading growth can make it less conspicuous than familiar garden geraniums.
186|Australian backyard mosquito has white-banded legs, a pale ring on its mouthparts and lyre-shaped markings on its back. It breeds in small water collections, including tree holes and containers around homes. Adults may bite during the day in shaded places. Originally associated with Australasia, it has also been detected in California, where container habitats can provide suitable places for it to reproduce.
187|Australian brake is a fern with large fronds divided into many narrow green segments. Brown stalks rise from a short base, and lines of spore-producing structures form along the undersides of the leaf edges. It grows naturally in Australasian forest and stream habitats and is also cultivated. Looking beneath a mature frond can reveal features that are less obvious from its overall shape.
188|Australian cheesewood is an evergreen tree with glossy, wavy-edged leaves and fragrant white flowers. Orange capsules open to expose sticky seeds that birds can carry away. Native to eastern Australia, it has become weedy in some places outside its natural range. Its attractive foliage and scent explain its garden use, while bird-dispersed seeds help it move beyond planted sites.
189|Australian eelgrass is a freshwater plant with long, ribbon-like leaves rooted in the bottom. It spreads through runners and can form dense underwater beds. Female flowers reach the surface, and their stalks coil after pollination. Despite the common name, it is not a marine seagrass. Introduced populations occur in places including California's Delta, where its underwater growth can change the structure of aquatic habitat.
190|Australian fingergrass has a seed head shaped like a small windmill, with narrow branches spreading outward in a roughly horizontal plane. It forms low clumps with narrow leaves, and the flowering branches darken as they mature. Native to Australia, it responds to rainfall and grows on a range of soils. The spoke-like head is its clearest feature when seen among other grasses.
191|Australian lovegrass is a loosely tufted perennial with narrow leaves and open, branching flower heads. Small, layered flower clusters sit along the fine branches, and hairs near the leaf joints can help with identification. It is native to eastern and southeastern Australia, where it grows in woodland and pasture settings. It should not be confused with other lovegrasses that have different ranges and impacts.
192|Australian redcedar can become a very large rainforest tree, with leaves divided into pointed leaflets and new growth often tinged red or pink. Older trees develop rough bark and may have broad buttresses at the base. Woody fruits release winged seeds. Its valuable timber led to extensive historical logging, and trees growing in open sites can look quite different from tall forest-grown specimens.
193|Australian redclaw crayfish is a large freshwater crayfish, often blue-green, with a distinctive red patch on the claws of mature males. It comes from northern Australia and nearby regions, using streams, lakes and seasonally changing waters. Widely farmed for food, it can escape cultivation. It eats a variety of plant and animal material, making its establishment relevant to the waters and species it encounters.
194|Australian saltbush is a low, spreading plant that can form a mat over dry or salty ground. Its gray-green leaves have a fine coating, especially beneath, and its seed-bearing structures become fleshy and red. It has been planted for forage and erosion control. Animals can spread its seeds, and it has established outside cultivation in places including coastal California.
195|Australian teatree is a shrub or small tree with gray-green leaves, white flowers and persistent woody seed capsules. Older plants may have twisted trunks and textured bark. Native to coastal southeastern Australia, it has been planted as a screen and windbreak and has become invasive in some other regions. Newer botanical references may list it as Gaudium laevigatum rather than Leptospermum laevigatum.
196|Australian tortoise beetle is a rounded brown beetle associated with eucalyptus. Adults and larvae feed on leaves, often leaving curved or semicircular notches along the edges. Adults commonly feed at night and shelter beneath bark during the day. Introduced populations occur in California. Mature trees can often tolerate some feeding, so the presence of beetles and the severity of damage should be assessed separately.
197|Australian tubeworm builds hard, pale tubes in brackish water. A feathery crown extends from each tube to collect food, while large colonies can form reef-like structures on submerged surfaces. It also grows on boats and marine infrastructure, which can help it travel. Despite its common name, its original native range is uncertain; the name should not be treated as proof of an Australian origin.
198|Australian waterbuttons is a small plant with finely divided leaves and tiny, cream to yellow flower heads that resemble buttons. The heads lack the showy outer petals of many familiar daisies. Stems can root where they touch suitable ground. Native to Australia and New Zealand, it also occurs in lawns, roadsides and other disturbed places elsewhere, often remaining close to the soil surface.
199|Austrian pine has stiff, dark green needles held in pairs. Young trees are often roughly pyramid-shaped, while older trees develop broader crowns, deeply furrowed bark and brown cones. Native across parts of Europe, northwestern Africa and western Asia, it has been widely planted for screens and windbreaks. Its paired needles and mature bark are useful features when comparing it with other planted pines.
200|Austrian yellowcress is an upright perennial with small, four-petaled yellow flowers and toothed leaves that clasp the stem. Rounded seed pods follow the flowers. It can spread through both seeds and creeping roots, allowing patches to expand in damp fields, pastures and river margins. In parts of the western United States, dense growth can compete with other vegetation, including desirable pasture plants.
