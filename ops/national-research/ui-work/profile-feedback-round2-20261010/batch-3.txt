101|Sipha maydis is a dark, glossy aphid with stiff hairs, often found near the bases of grass leaves. It feeds on grasses and cereal crops, including wheat, barley, oats and corn. Colonies can injure leaves, and the species can also transmit plant viruses. Its nearly black or brown appearance helps distinguish it from some common green aphids, though a close examination is still important.
102|Tinocallis ulmiparvifoliae is an aphid associated with Chinese elm. Winged individuals have pale blue-green bodies with waxy markings, and colonies feed on the leaves. Originally associated with Asia, it has also been recorded in Florida as an introduced insect. Its host is a useful clue, but fine anatomical features are needed to distinguish it confidently from other elm-feeding aphids.
103|Tuberocephalus sakurae is a tiny aphid associated with cherry trees. Feeding can cause leaves to become red and crumpled, creating sheltered spaces for the insects. Research from Japan describes individuals only around a millimeter and a half long. The altered leaves may be easier to notice than the aphids themselves, but similar damage can have other causes and does not establish the species on its own.
104|Uroleucon picridis is a shiny reddish-brown aphid associated with oxtongues in the genus Picris. Colonies may gather on flowering stems. Dark antennae and rear tubes contrast with a paler, yellowish tail-like projection at the end of the body. These details help specialists distinguish it from related aphids, but its host and color alone are not enough for a reliable identification.
105|This aphid parasite is a tiny wasp that uses aphids as a nursery for its young. A female Aphidius ervi lays an egg inside an aphid; the developing wasp eventually leaves a dry, swollen shell called a mummy and emerges through a round hole. It attacks aphids including potato and foxglove aphids and is used in biological control. Its presence can therefore reflect pest management rather than a new pest problem.
106|Apple aphid gathers on young leaves and shoots, including the vigorous upright shoots of apple trees. The green insects feed on plant sap and produce sticky honeydew that can support sooty mold. Heavy colonies may weaken young growth, while predators can help limit their numbers. The aphid survives winter as dark eggs on woody growth, ready to hatch as fresh leaves become available.
107|Apple blossom, Cassia javanica, is a tropical tree grown for clusters of pink flowers. Its leaves are divided into pairs of leaflets, and its fruit develops into long, cylindrical pods. Different forms within the species vary in appearance, which can complicate identification. The common name refers to the flowers' ornamental appeal; this is a member of the legume family, not an apple tree.
108|This form of apple blossom belongs to the Cassia javanica group of tropical flowering trees. It has pink flowers, leaves divided into paired leaflets and long pods. Botanical references have treated its rank differently, including as a variety or subspecies. Pink flowers alone cannot distinguish it from related forms, so a precise identification depends on additional plant features and the reference being used.
109|Apple mealybug is a soft-bodied insect covered in white wax. It feeds on several woody plants, not just apples, and may hide in bark crevices where colonies are easy to miss. White egg sacs and sticky honeydew can reveal its presence. It is also a known vector of little cherry virus 2, making accurate identification particularly important in affected fruit-growing areas.
110|Applemint is a perennial herb with rounded, wrinkled, hairy leaves and a fruity scent. Small, pale flowers grow in spikes above the foliage. It spreads through underground stems, which can carry it beyond the edges of a garden bed. Native to parts of Europe and the Mediterranean region, it also grows outside cultivation in moist ground. Its soft leaves help distinguish it from smoother-leaved mints.
111|Apple of Peru has pale blue, bell-shaped flowers followed by fruit enclosed in a papery covering. This annual member of the nightshade family comes from South America and can grow quickly in fertile, disturbed ground. It spreads by seed and may appear as a garden escape or field weed. Despite the familiar-looking fruit covering, it should not be confused with edible groundcherries.
112|Arabian jasmine is an evergreen shrub or scrambling climber with glossy, oval leaves and strongly scented white flowers. The flowers may turn pink as they age, and cultivated forms can have one or several layers of petals. Despite its name, its native range includes areas from Bhutan to India. It is widely grown in warm climates for its flowers and fragrance.
113|Arabian schismus is a small annual grass that grows in dry, open places. After rain, its fine leaves and stems can fill spaces between desert shrubs; when dry, that growth can help fire move through areas that once had little continuous fuel. It closely resembles another introduced schismus grass, so small flower details are important for separating the two species.
114|This aradid flat bug lives around decaying wood and feeds on fungi. Its flattened body suits the narrow spaces beneath bark and within woody material. Brachyrhynchus membranaceus has records in both the Old World and the Western Hemisphere, and has sometimes affected cultivated bracket fungi. Finding it in wood does not mean it is attacking a healthy tree; its feeding is associated with fungal growth.
115|Araucaria aphid feeds on young growth of Araucaria trees. Wingless individuals are broadly oval and yellow to orange, measuring only around one to one and a half millimeters long. It has been recorded in tropical regions where its host trees grow. Its small size makes close examination useful, and its wider distribution should not be treated as a clear map of its original native range.
116|Araucaria black scale forms a small, round, dark covering on its host plant. The cover is usually brown to black, with darker remains of earlier skins near the center. Despite the name, it can feed on plants beyond Araucaria. Like other armored scales, the insect spends much of its life protected beneath this cover, making the covering more visible than the animal itself.
117|Arborescent pricklypear is a Mexican cactus that can grow into a large shrub or small tree. Its gray-green pads carry white hairs and spines, while older growth develops a trunk. Yellow flowers are followed by fleshy fruit. Broken pads can take root, allowing plants to spread from discarded garden material. It has escaped cultivation in places including Australia, where dense growth can become a management problem.
118|Argentine ants are small brown ants often seen moving in long, busy trails. They form networks of shallow nests with multiple queens and can spread through gardens, buildings and natural areas. Native to South America, they have become established in many other regions. They collect sugary honeydew from sap-feeding insects and can displace other ants where their colonies become dominant.
119|Argentine black and white tegu is a large, strong-bodied lizard with a banded tail. Young animals often have greenish heads. It eats a wide range of foods and can shelter in burrows during cold weather. Established populations in Florida are a concern because tegus eat native wildlife, including eggs and young animals; documented prey include alligator eggs and young gopher tortoises.
120|Argentine pepperweed is a hairy member of the mustard family with divided lower leaves and simpler leaves higher on the stem. Its small white flowers develop into flattened pods with a notch at the tip. The lower leaves may disappear as the plant matures, changing its overall appearance. Looking at both the remaining leaves and the seed pods helps distinguish it from other pepperweeds.
121|Aonidiella taxus is an armored scale found on plants such as yews, plum-yews and Podocarpus. A pale, partly translucent cover hides the reddish female beneath it. Feeding on leaf undersides can be associated with yellow patches. The young insects are mobile before settling down, and infested nursery plants can carry them to new locations even when the small scales are not immediately obvious.
122|Dynaspidiotus pseudomeyeri is an armored scale associated with conifers, including arborvitae. Its raised, oval cover is yellowish when young and becomes browner with age. The insect feeds beneath this protective layer, which can make it look like a small bump on the plant. Natural enemies include tiny wasps and mites, although their presence does not guarantee that a particular infestation will remain harmless.
123|Gymnaspis aechmeae is a tiny armored scale sometimes called flyspeck scale. Its dark covering can resemble a speck of dirt on bromeliads and other ornamental plants, including those grown in greenhouses. The female remains within the skin from an earlier growth stage and produces live young that can crawl to new feeding sites. Infested plants provide a route for it to move between locations.
124|Howardia biclavis is an armored scale whose rounded, pale covering can become hidden by the host plant's outer tissue. This unusual feature has led to the name mining scale, although it is not a leaf-mining insect. It feeds on a range of woody plants. Because the covering blends into the plant surface, colonies may be less noticeable than exposed scales on leaves or stems.
125|Lepidosaphes pinnaeformis is an armored scale associated with orchids. Its brown, elongated covering resembles a tiny mussel shell and may occur on leaves or stems. Feeding can discolor affected tissue. Newly hatched scales can crawl before settling, while established insects remain protected beneath their covers. Moving an infested orchid can transport the pest without the small colonies being noticed immediately.
126|Lepidosaphes yanagicola is an armored scale with a brown, elongated cover. It occurs on woody plants, including the corky ridges of burning-bush stems, and has also been called firebush scale. Several scales use similar hosts, so the plant's name alone is not enough to identify it. In particular, it should not be confused with the different insect commonly called euonymus scale.
127|Neopinnaspis harperi is a small armored scale that can hide in bark crevices on woody plants. Its narrow, fairly flat cover is tan with a darker end, concealing a pinkish female underneath. The insect is only around one to two millimeters long. Available California accounts report limited known damage, and its small size and sheltered position make careful inspection more informative than a distant view of the plant.
128|Parlatoria camelliae is an armored scale found on camellias and other hosts. Its pale to gray covering has a darker patch made from earlier shed skins. Colonies can occur on either side of a leaf, often near veins, and feeding may discolor the foliage. Reports from places such as Galicia document its presence, but identification still requires attention to the insect rather than leaf discoloration alone.
129|Rutherfordia major is an armored scale associated with lychee and other tropical woody plants. Its initially pale covering may become covered by plant tissue and appear reddish or dark, helping it blend into bark. It has been introduced to places including the Galapagos. Movement of host plants is relevant to its spread, while the full history of its original distribution remains less certain.
130|Selenaspidus albus is an armored scale with a small, white, disk-shaped cover. A yellow-brown patch sits slightly off-center, marking the remains of earlier growth stages. It is associated with plants including Euphorbia. The covering is less than two millimeters across, so individual insects are easy to overlook; heavier colonies are more conspicuous and can warrant closer inspection of the host plant.
131|Selenaspidus articulatus has a thin, partly transparent cover with a pale rim and an off-center darker patch. It feeds on citrus and many other plants, often on exposed upper leaf surfaces. Heavy infestations can cause yellowing and affect leaves or fruit. The name West Indian red scale appears in references, but a close look is needed to separate it from similar armored scales.
132|Unaspis citri is often noticed as a snowy white coating on citrus trunks and branches. That appearance comes largely from the white covers of immature males; females have darker, shell-shaped covers. Heavy feeding can weaken trees, contribute to branch dieback and cause bark to split. Its concentration on woody parts of the tree helps distinguish citrus snow scale from some scales found mainly on leaves.
133|This armored scale is listed as Andaspis punicae, though newer references also place it in Lepidosaphes. Its brown, elongated covering resembles a small shell on woody hosts, including lychee. Heavy populations may reduce plant vigor or damage branches. Changes in the scientific name can make records harder to connect, so both names are useful when checking the research behind an identification.
134|Fiorinia proboscidaria is a small armored scale sometimes called snout scale. Its elongated covering is glossy and golden-brown, and the female remains protected within an earlier shed skin. It occurs on citrus and other plants. Several related scales look much alike, so the cover's general shape is only a starting point; reliable identification depends on more detailed examination.
135|Aroma is a thorny shrub or small tree with finely divided leaves and unusual hanging flowers: pink or mauve at one end and yellow at the other. Its pods become twisted as they mature. It can spread through both seed and shoots from its roots, producing dense thickets. Native across parts of the Old World, it has become invasive in some places outside that range.
136|Arrocillo is a coarse perennial grass that forms clumps with broad leaves and many spreading flowering branches. Small, rounded flower clusters line those branches. It grows in warm places such as clearings, plantations and other disturbed ground. Its natural distribution includes a broad area of the Americas, so a record in this region does not automatically mean the grass is an introduced species.
137|Arrow bamboo has upright canes and long, broad leaves that make it popular as a screen. Beneath the soil, spreading stems can carry new shoots well beyond the original planting. It can form dense patches where it escapes cultivation. Records from places including Maryland and New Zealand illustrate why its underground growth matters when assessing a planting near neighboring gardens or natural vegetation.
138|Arrowleaf elephant ear has large, arrow-shaped leaves held on long stalks that meet the leaf at its base. Thick underground growth and new shoots help it expand into patches. It favors warm, moist places and can grow along streams. In parts of Florida, escaped plants are considered invasive, where their large leaves and dense growth can crowd other vegetation.
139|Artist's pallet is grown for its heart- to arrow-shaped leaves, patterned in combinations of green, white, pink and red. It grows from underground tubers and produces small flowers grouped on a fleshy spike within a pale surrounding leaf-like structure. Native to tropical America, it is widely cultivated. Its colorful foliage is ornamental rather than edible, and the plant's tissues can cause irritation.
140|Diplosoma listerianum is a colonial sea squirt that forms thin, soft sheets over submerged surfaces. Many tiny filtering animals share the same gelatinous covering. Colonies grow on docks, boat hulls and natural surfaces, giving them opportunities to travel with marine traffic. They can cover other organisms as they spread, but the long history of transport makes the species' original native range difficult to establish.
141|Polyandrocarpa zorritensis is a colonial sea squirt made up of small, club-shaped individuals connected by root-like strands. Each has its own outer covering and two openings used in filter feeding. Colonies occur on docks, boats and natural surfaces such as mangrove roots, and can overgrow neighboring organisms. It was described from Peru, but that alone does not establish where it originally evolved.
142|Ash whitefly feeds beneath the leaves of trees and shrubs, including pears, pomegranates and citrus. Adults are small and white; immature insects have distinctive waxy structures and may carry tiny droplets. Feeding and honeydew can harm foliage and encourage sooty mold. In California, introduced natural enemies greatly reduced a once-serious outbreak, making this species a notable example of successful biological control.
143|Ashy cinquefoil has yellow flowers and divided, toothed leaves whose hairy undersides give them a grayish appearance. Its stems are also hairy and rise above the lower foliage. Native to Eurasia, it occurs in North American roadsides, fields and open woodland. Several cinquefoils have similar yellow flowers, so leaf shape and the gray undersides are useful details to examine together.
144|Ashy gecko is a tiny lizard that hides among leaf litter, bark, rocks and sheltered spaces near buildings. Young animals have strong bands and reddish tails, while adults become more muted and speckled. It has been introduced to Florida, where it can be confused with other small geckos. The change in pattern as it grows means young and adult animals may look surprisingly different.
145|Ashy pipewort grows as a small tuft of narrow leaves on wet ground. Thin stalks rise above the leaves and end in compact, button-shaped flower heads. It occurs in wet margins and rice-growing settings, where plants without flowers may resemble a small grass. The round heads are a more useful recognition feature than the leaves alone.
146|Asia minor bluegrass is an annual grass with a narrow, compact flower head lined with fine bristles. Its leaves feel rough, and lower stem joints may take root where they touch suitable ground. It grows in damp, disturbed places. Despite the common name, it belongs to Polypogon rather than the bluegrass genus Poa, so flower details are helpful when checking an identification.
147|Asian bush mosquito has pale-banded legs and golden markings on its back. It uses small pools of water, including rock pools, tree holes, tires and other containers, and can thrive in relatively cool, wooded settings near homes. Its ability to use artificial containers helps it spread. The species can carry pathogens under some conditions, but finding it does not establish local disease transmission.
148|Asian chestnut gall wasp causes rounded swellings on the buds, leaves and shoots of true chestnut trees. These galls start green or pink and later become woody and brown, sometimes remaining after the wasps have left. Heavy infestations can reduce shoot growth and nut production. The adult wasp is tiny, so the galls are usually the first sign people notice.
149|Asian citrus psyllid is a small sap-feeding insect often seen with its body tilted upward as it feeds on tender citrus growth. Young insects are yellow-orange and produce curled, waxy tubes. Feeding can distort new leaves, but its greater importance is its ability to transmit the bacteria associated with citrus greening disease. Identifying the insect accurately helps distinguish that risk from ordinary leaf damage.
150|Asian cockroach usually lives outdoors in leaf litter, mulch and similar sheltered places. It closely resembles the German cockroach, but flies readily and is attracted to lights, especially around dusk. Adults may enter buildings from outside. That behavior matters when interpreting a sighting: one flying into a lit room does not necessarily indicate the kind of indoor infestation associated with its lookalike.
