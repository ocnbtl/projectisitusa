1|Absinthium has silvery leaves, small yellow flower heads and a strong scent. Its leaves are deeply divided and pale on both sides, unlike common mugwort's greener upper surfaces. This perennial herb comes from Eurasia and has long been grown for its aromatic qualities, including its use in absinthe. It also grows outside gardens along roads, railways and other disturbed ground, returning from the same plant each year.
2|Despite the name achicoria azul, this plant has yellow flowers. Slender stems branch above a cluster of toothed or lobed leaves, carrying small flower heads that later release seeds with white hairs. The leaves become smaller farther up the stems. A member of the daisy family, it grows in warm, open places such as dry riverbeds, rocky ground and cultivated land.
3|Only a few millimeters long, this marine worm lives among seaweeds and other growth on the seafloor. The sides of its body curl downward, giving it a folded shape. Algae inside its body help give it a greenish or yellow-brown color and provide some of its food; it also eats tiny animals and algae. It is known from the northeastern Atlantic and nearby seas, with records in the Gulf of Maine as well.
4|The active gray pine needle aphid is a slender insect that feeds on pine needles. Its long legs, olive-gray body and light coating of wax can make it hard to spot among the foliage. Feeding may turn needles yellow. The aphids also release a sticky liquid called honeydew, which can support dark sooty mold. Lady beetles, lacewings and other predators feed on them, while winged adults can move to other trees.
5|Acuminate scale sits flat against a leaf, where its pale green body can blend into the surface. This small sap-feeding insect has an oval to triangular shape that narrows toward the head. It feeds on several kinds of plants, including mango, gardenia and ornamental foliage plants. Related scales can look very similar, so its color and host plant are useful clues but cannot confirm its identity on their own.
6|A patch of white, wool-like wax on a pine twig may be hiding these tiny insects. Pineus boerneri is an adelgid, a close relative of aphids that feeds on pine trees. Its young can move to new feeding sites, while the insects and eggs shelter beneath the wax. Heavy infestations can damage trees. Other pine adelgids look very similar, so identifying the insect takes more than recognizing the white covering.
7|Aegean wallflower grows in gardens, rocky crevices and even cracks in old walls. Its fragrant flowers have four petals and are often yellow or orange, although cultivated varieties come in other colors. Narrow leaves grow along stems that become woody at the base. The plant can live for several years, but gardeners often grow it over two seasons. Its ability to root in walls gives the common name a literal meaning.
8|African amaranth's name is misleading: the plant comes from South America. It grows low to the ground with narrow leaves and small green flowers clustered along its stems. Its tiny fruits have a rough, wrinkled surface, a useful detail for distinguishing it from other amaranths. This herb grows in disturbed places, where its green flowers are much less noticeable than those of many neighboring plants.
9|African asparagus fern climbs over nearby plants on thin, wiry stems. It has glossy, oval growths that look like leaves but are actually flattened stems, followed by small white flowers and orange-red berries. Despite its name, it belongs to the asparagus family rather than the ferns. Thickened roots store reserves underground, helping it grow back after its shoots die. Dense growth can shade other plants and leave little space for them to establish.
10|African bermudagrass spreads on long runners that grow across the soil and take root, gradually forming a dense patch. Several narrow flower spikes spread from each flowering stem like the points of a star. This warm-climate perennial is grown for livestock forage, ground cover and erosion control. It is closely related to common bermudagrass, but its growth and varieties differ. In pastures, it is often started from pieces of living stems.
11|African blackchin tilapia lives in warm waters, including coastal lagoons and estuaries where fresh and salt water mix. It feeds mainly on tiny algae and organic debris. The male has an unusual role in raising the young: he shelters the developing offspring in his mouth. Native to African coastal waters, the fish has been moved elsewhere through fish farming and other human activity, with populations recorded in Florida and Hawaii.
12|African bowstring hemp grows stiff, cylindrical leaves that taper to sharp points. Darker bands cross the gray-green leaves, which form a fan as the plant spreads from underground stems. This southern African succulent is widely grown as a houseplant, and mature plants may produce clusters of pale, fragrant flowers. Botanical references now often list it as Dracaena angolensis rather than Sansevieria cylindrica, the name still used in many older records.
13|African boxthorn is a shrub with long thorns and small leaves clustered along its branches. Pale mauve or cream flowers develop into red berries. As it grows, its many branches can form a dense thicket several meters tall. Originally from southern Africa, it has become established elsewhere in open and coastal areas, pastures and roadsides. The long, rigid thorns are an important feature to notice before getting close.
14|African bristlegrass grows in leafy clumps topped by long, narrow flower heads. Golden bristles surround the tiny flowers, while the leaves range from green to blue-gray. The size of a plant can vary considerably: some forms make small clumps, while selected pasture varieties grow tall and leafy. It is a perennial, growing back over more than one season, and several varieties are used as livestock forage in tropical regions.
15|The African clawed frog spends almost all its life in water. Eyes on top of its flattened head help it look upward, while powerful, webbed hind feet propel it through the water. Three toes on each hind foot have the dark claws that give it its name; the smaller front feet have no webbing. Native to southern Africa, this smooth-skinned frog is also familiar from aquariums and scientific research.
16|African cornflag has long, sword-shaped leaves and tall stems with rows of orange flowers. Each flower is tubular, with one upper petal much longer than the others; some forms have yellow flowers. This South African member of the iris family grows from a swollen underground stem called a corm, where it survives the dry season. Grown as an ornamental, it has escaped gardens in some regions, including California.
17|African feathergrass grows in large clumps, with arching leaves and long, narrow flower heads surrounded by fine bristles. Underground stems spread outward and send up new shoots, allowing a clump to become a broad patch. It grows along waterways, roadsides and neglected pasture. Where it spreads densely, it can crowd out other vegetation. Pieces of underground stem can also travel in moved soil or discarded garden material.
18|African fig fly has bright red eyes and pale stripes edged in black along its head and back. Those markings help distinguish this tiny insect from other fruit flies. Its young usually develop in damaged or rotting fruit, but they can also infest ripening figs and some berries. Originally from Africa, it has spread through warm regions and into parts of North America. Despite the name, figs are only one of its food sources.
19|African jewelfish is a small cichlid that hunts tiny fish and invertebrates among rocks and vegetation. It can live in fresh or brackish water and tolerate low oxygen levels. Native to parts of Africa, it has established populations in Florida, likely following aquarium releases or fish-farm escapes. Older Florida reports often called it Hemichromis bimaculatus, a different jewel cichlid, so the scientific name matters when comparing records.
20|African jointgrass has long stems that bend across the ground and take root at their lower joints. Its narrow leaves sometimes develop reddish tips, and its flower spikes are small and easy to miss. This perennial tolerates wet, poorly drained soil. Farmers grow selected varieties for livestock because they keep growing into cooler weather when many other warm-season grasses slow down. Outside pastures, it can form thick patches in suitable wet ground.
21|African mustard has small pink to violet flowers, hairy branching stems and long, slender seedpods. Its lower leaves are more deeply divided than the smaller leaves farther up the plant. Like other mustards, each flower has four petals. This annual comes from parts of the Mediterranean region and western Asia and also grows beyond that range on disturbed ground. Flowers and mature pods together are useful when comparing it with similar roadside plants.
22|African oil palm supplies two different oils: palm oil comes from the fruit's flesh, while palm-kernel oil comes from the seed inside. The tree has a sturdy trunk, long feather-shaped leaves and heavy clusters of red or orange fruit. Old leaf bases mark the trunk, and spines line the leaf stalks. Native to tropical Africa, it is now widely cultivated in warm regions as a major crop.
23|African olive is an evergreen tree or shrub with a dense canopy and narrow leaves growing in opposite pairs. The leaf tips often curve into a small hook, and their undersides are less silvery than those of familiar orchard olives. Small cream flowers develop into dark fruits. Planted for hedges and ornament, it has escaped cultivation in some regions, where its dense shade can change the vegetation growing beneath it.
24|African pepperwort is a mustard-family herb whose tiny flowers are easy to miss. As it develops, small, flat seedpods line its branching stems, each with a shallow notch at the tip. Lower leaves are broader and toothed, while upper leaves are narrower. It grows in cultivated and disturbed ground and may last for one season or longer. Its pods and leaf hairs help distinguish it from similar pepperworts.
25|African rock python is a large, muscular snake with irregular dark patches on a tan, gray or brown body. A spear-shaped marking on its head and small heat-sensing pits along its lips are useful features. This nonvenomous snake constricts its prey, which includes birds and mammals, and often lives near water. Females lay eggs and coil around them while they develop, protecting the clutch until the young hatch.
26|African senna has bright yellow flowers that open beneath dark buds on upright flower spikes. Its long leaves divide into pairs of smaller leaflets, and flat pods follow the flowers. The foliage has a distinctive scent, often compared with buttered popcorn. This tropical African plant can become a large shrub in warm climates. In colder places, it is more often grown as a seasonal garden ornamental.
27|African tamarisk is a shrub or small tree with slender branches and tiny, overlapping leaves. Clusters of white to pale pink flowers grow along the branches, often on wood from the previous year. It belongs to a group of trees associated with riverbanks, shores and other open places near fresh or brackish water. Several tamarisks look very similar, making close-up leaf and flower details important for identification.
28|African tuliptree produces large orange-red flowers above broad leaves divided into smaller leaflets. The flowers open into cups, often with a yellow edge. Native to tropical Africa, the tree has been widely planted for shade and its colorful blooms. After flowering, long woody seed cases split into two halves and release many thin, winged seeds. The flowers and open seed cases are especially distinctive features.
29|African warthog has a broad head, curved tusks and a mane along its neck and back. The fleshy bumps on its face give it its name and are more prominent in males. This wild pig mainly eats grasses, roots and other plant material. It favors open country and shelters in burrows. Long legs and a sparse coat give it a noticeably different shape from a domestic pig.
30|Air yam climbs over shrubs and trees on twining stems with broad, heart-shaped leaves. Round, potato-like growths form where the leaves join the stems. These are bulbils: small structures that can fall to the ground and grow into new vines. Underground parts also survive when the shoots die back. Despite the resemblance of its bulbils to potatoes, this perennial belongs to the yam family, not the potato family.
31|Alder rust causes orange-yellow patches on the undersides of alder leaves. Spots may also appear above, and badly affected leaves can curl, turn brown and fall early. The fungus releases spores that travel on the wind. Its life cycle can involve both alder and larch, although it can also repeatedly infect alder. Related rust fungi cause similar symptoms, so leaf spots alone cannot confirm which species is responsible.
32|Aleppo pine has fine, light green needles that usually grow in pairs. As the tree ages, its crown often becomes open and irregular. Woody cones can stay attached for years, leaving cones of different ages on the same tree. This Mediterranean evergreen tolerates heat, dry conditions and poor soil and is widely planted in warm landscapes. Its needles, persistent cones and overall shape help distinguish it from other pines.
33|Alexander palm has a slender gray trunk marked with rings and a smooth green section just below its crown. Its long leaves divide into many leaflets with blunt or jagged tips. Small fruits ripen red beneath the leaves. Native to northeastern Australian rainforest, it is also grown in tropical gardens. The shape of the leaflet tips is particularly useful when comparing it with other palms of similar size and appearance.
34|Alexandrian laurel grows along tropical shores, often on sandy or rocky ground. Its thick, glossy leaves grow in pairs and have many fine, closely spaced veins. White, fragrant flowers with yellow centers develop into round fruits. A short trunk and spreading branches support a broad canopy, which has made the tree popular for shade. Its leaf veins and rounded fruits are useful features to look for together.
35|Alfalfa is a perennial forage crop with three-part leaves and clusters of small, usually purple flowers. A deep root system allows it to grow back after cutting or winter dormancy. Bacteria living in small swellings on its roots help it obtain nitrogen from the air. Beyond farm fields, it also grows along roadsides and on disturbed ground. Its coiled seedpods help distinguish it from similar-looking clovers.
36|This is the commonly cultivated form of alfalfa, grown for hay and livestock forage. It has three-part leaves, purple to blue-violet flowers and seedpods that curl into tight coils. New stems grow from a lasting base above a deep root system, allowing the plant to regrow after cutting. It can also persist around field edges and disturbed ground. The coiled pods help distinguish it from yellow-flowered relatives.
37|Gracilaria vermiculophylla forms branching tangles of narrow, rounded strands in sheltered coastal water. It can attach to shells and other hard surfaces or gather in loose mats over mudflats and seagrass beds. Though classified as a red seaweed, its visible color varies. Native to the northwestern Pacific, it has spread to estuaries elsewhere. Dense tangles can crowd vegetation while also providing shelter for small animals, with effects that vary by location.
38|Grateloupia doryphora is a red seaweed described from Peru and recorded on rocky shores there. It belongs to a group of algae that can be difficult to tell apart by appearance. Its name was once used for some seaweeds later identified as Grateloupia turuturu. That naming history is important: reports under the older name may describe a different species, so they cannot all be treated as records of this one.
39|Algerian sea lavender has a low cluster of spoon-shaped leaves beneath branching stems of tiny pale pink flowers. This Mediterranean perennial grows in salty coastal places, including the upper edges of salt marshes. In parts of California, it has spread into marsh vegetation. Related sea lavenders have sometimes been confused in older records, so the leaves and flowers both matter when checking an identification.
40|Alligatorweed grows across wet ground and shallow water. Smooth leaves grow in pairs along its stems, and small white flower heads rise above them on short stalks. In water, hollow stems help support floating patches; on land, it can form a lower, denser cover. The stems take root at their joints, and broken pieces can start new plants. This helps it spread along shorelines, drainage channels and damp ground.
41|Rows of tiny punctures on onion, leek or garlic leaves can be an early sign of allium leafminer. Adult female flies make these marks, while their young tunnel down inside the plant. Feeding may distort the leaves, and larvae or brown pupae can remain hidden between leaf layers or near the bulb. The adult is a small gray fly with yellow on its head, but its leaf damage is often noticed first.
42|Allspice comes from one plant, not a mixture of spices. The dried, unripe berries of this tropical evergreen tree have a flavor often compared with cinnamon, cloves and nutmeg. The tree has glossy, scented leaves in opposite pairs and clusters of small white flowers. Its fruits darken as they ripen. Native to parts of Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean, it has long been grown elsewhere for spice and ornament.
43|Almond moth caterpillars feed inside stored foods and leave silk that can bind pieces together. Nuts, dried fruit, seeds, flour and grain can all support them, so the name describes only part of their diet. The adults are small gray moths with faint wing markings and can resemble other pantry moths. Webbing in a food container may be more noticeable than the moth itself.
44|Aloe aphids hide where the leaves meet at the base of an aloe. Their pale waxy covering can make a colony look like mealybugs, but small sap-feeding aphids live underneath it. They also feed on some related succulents, including Haworthia. The insects have traveled with cultivated plants and been recorded in greenhouses far from their probable African origin. Looking closely beneath the leaf bases can reveal colonies missed on exposed leaves.
45|Alsike clover has rounded flower heads that change from pale to pink as the individual flowers age. Its leaves have three leaflets, usually without the pale markings seen on many other clovers. The stems grow mostly upright rather than creeping and rooting along the ground. This short-lived perennial has been grown for forage and often occurs in cool, moist fields and meadows. Its leaves and changing flower colors are useful identification clues.
46|Alyssum strigosum is a small annual mustard from parts of southern Europe and western Asia. Its tiny fruits carry both star-shaped hairs and coarser, forked hairs, features that help separate it from related alyssums. These details may need magnification to see clearly. Some older North American records used this name for another species, A. simplex, which makes checking the original identification especially important.
47|Amaranth aphids can gather around a plant's roots and stem base as well as its leaves and flowers. These small olive or reddish-brown insects feed on amaranths, often called pigweeds, and ants may attend their colonies. Their sheltered feeding sites can make them easy to miss during a quick look at the foliage. Similar aphids share these plants, so the host and body color alone do not confirm the species.
48|Amazon sailfin catfish has rows of hard body plates, a tall back fin and a sucker-like mouth beneath its head. This freshwater fish is familiar in the aquarium trade as a pleco and spends much of its time against the bottom or other surfaces. Dark spots on its belly help distinguish it from some related catfish. Similar species and hybrids can complicate identification, so the underside pattern should not be used alone.
49|Ambay pumpwood has large, deeply divided leaves on long stalks. Their pale, hairy undersides become visible when the leaves turn in the wind. This fast-growing South American tree often establishes on open or disturbed ground, where it can soon rise above smaller plants. Male and female flowers grow on separate trees. The broad leaves make it noticeable even when young, although related Cecropia trees can look similar.
50|Anisandrus maiche spends much of its life inside wood. Female beetles bore small tunnels and grow fungi there to feed their young. This tiny ambrosia beetle comes from Asia and has also been recorded in North America. Small holes in wood may draw attention to its presence, but similar beetles leave comparable signs. Identifying the insect requires a close look at the beetle itself.
